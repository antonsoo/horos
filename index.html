    What this experiment can tell you.

    A modern model beside an ancient text

    The circles represent three classes. Every object either belongs or does not belong to each class. “Every” and “no” have no violating instance; “some” and “some not” require an instance. “Some” means at least one, and can include all.

    Universal statements here are true when their subject class is empty. Existence is an additional, visible requirement. The checkboxes let you test this convention; they do not reproduce a complete historical theory of Aristotle’s existential assumptions. The third-figure passage actually takes an instance.

    The app checks these membership assignments, not the truth of claims about animals, ethics, science, or law. It does not model modality, relations, changing time, or all of ancient logic. A failed proposed conclusion does not imply that no other conclusion follows.

    Why eight places are enough

    Three memberships, each yes or no, give eight possible regions. All four kinds of statement used here ask only whether certain regions are empty or occupied. They never count objects.

    From any counterexample, however large, keep one representative of each occupied region. Every premise, conclusion, and class-existence condition keeps the same truth value. Conversely, each nonempty occupancy pattern is itself a finite world.

    There are 28 = 256 patterns. The completely empty universe is excluded, leaving 255. Checking all of them is exhaustive for this defined model, not merely a sample of small examples. The proof would not apply unchanged to sentences about “exactly two”, identity, or relationships.

    The pattern of an argument

    Greek “A belongs to every B” corresponds to English “Every B is A”. The source’s Γ is our C. In I.6 the source’s Π / Ρ / Σ become A / B / C; Ν is the selected instance. The translated letter names are a reading aid.
